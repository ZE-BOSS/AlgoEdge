# Video 3: order-flow tricks, Sarao and market making — what it would take

*16 Oct 2026*

## 1. What Navinder Sarao did, and whether it still works

Sarao traded E-mini S&P futures from home with modified off-the-shelf
software. The core technique was **layering/spoofing**: placing large sell
orders several ticks away from the price that he intended to cancel before
they filled, so that other participants read the book as heavier than it was
and the price leaned his way, then trading the move and cancelling. US
authorities charged him in 2015 in connection with the 2010 flash crash; he
pleaded guilty in 2016 to spoofing and wire fraud.

**It is not something to build, for three separate reasons:**

1. **It is a crime.** Spoofing — bidding or offering with the intent to cancel
   before execution — is expressly prohibited in the US (Dodd-Frank §747,
   Commodity Exchange Act) and is market manipulation under the UK/EU Market
   Abuse Regulation. Crypto exchanges ban it in their terms and close accounts.
2. **It is detected automatically.** Exchanges and regulators run surveillance
   on order-to-trade ratios, cancel timing relative to price moves and layered
   size away from the touch. That is exactly the pattern this produces.
3. **It no longer works mechanically.** Modern HFT market makers cancel and
   requote in microseconds and price large resting size as likely to vanish;
   the "phantom wall" effect that Sarao exploited is now discounted by the very
   participants it was meant to fool.

I won't design or code it. The rest of this file is the **legal** version of
"make money from both buyers and sellers": being a market maker.

## 2. Market making: what it is

You post a bid below and an ask above a fair price. When an eager seller hits
your bid and an eager buyer lifts your ask, you earn the spread. Your profit
per round trip is roughly

    spread captured  +  maker rebate (if any)
    -  fees
    -  adverse selection   (you get filled just before price moves against you)
    -  inventory loss      (you are left holding a position that moves against you)

The last two are the whole game. A naive maker earns the spread on quiet
fills and gives it all back, and more, on the fills that come just before a
move — because the informed traders are the ones who choose to trade with you.

## 4. The models you need

1. **Fair value** - not the mid. The *microprice* (mid weighted by the
   imbalance of size at the best bid and ask) plus short-term order-flow
   imbalance; optionally a lead from a more liquid venue (e.g. BTC perp
   leading altcoin perps).
2. **Quote placement - Avellaneda–Stoikov** (2008) and its bounded-inventory
   extension (Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia, 2013):
   - reservation price `r = s - q·γ·σ²·(T - t)` (shift your centre away from
     the side you are already long/short on, `q` = inventory);
   - optimal half-spread `δ = ½·γ·σ²·(T - t) + (1/γ)·ln(1 + γ/k)`;
   - `γ` risk aversion, `σ` volatility, `k` how fast fill probability decays
     with distance from the touch - all estimated from your own recorded data.
3. **Adverse-selection filters** - widen or pull quotes when toxicity rises:
   trade-flow imbalance, VPIN-style volume buckets, large prints, funding/news
   events, volatility bursts.
4. **Inventory limits and hedging** - hard position caps; skew quotes as
   inventory builds; hedge in a correlated, more liquid market when the cap is
   near.
5. **Queue model** - for a fill simulator you must estimate where your order
   sits in the queue; a backtest that assumes you fill whenever price touches
   your level overstates profit enormously (same lesson as AlgoEdge's
   "stops fill exactly at the stop" error, in reverse).

## 5. Infrastructure

| Piece | What | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Market data | Websocket L2 book + trades, every update recorded to disk | Your own recordings become the backtest data |
| Historical data | Hyperliquid's public archive (requester-pays S3) or a vendor such as Tardis.dev | Needed for a proper simulator before risking money |
| Simulator | Event-driven, replays recorded book + trades with a queue model, fees, latency | The single most important piece; most retail MM projects die here |
| Quoting engine | asyncio Python first (fine at Hyperliquid's latency), post-only orders, cancel/replace with rate-limit budgeting | Rust/C++ only if latency ever becomes the binding constraint |
| Risk | Position caps, max quote size, loss-per-hour and per-day kill switches, stale-data kill (no book update for N ms → pull everything) | Non-negotiable |
| Hosting | Small VPS in the venue's region (AWS Tokyo for Hyperliquid) | Measure latency from it before choosing |
| Monitoring | P&L split into spread earned, fees, adverse selection, inventory; Telegram alerts | You must see *why* it makes or loses money |

## 6. Implementation plan

1. **Record (2 weeks)** - collector on a Tokyo VPS recording L2 + trades for
   ~5 candidate perps (a liquid one as the control, 3-4 long-tail ones).
2. **Measure** - spread distribution, depth, fill-probability-vs-distance
   (`k`), volatility (`σ`), toxicity after fills. Answer: is there any market
   where expected spread capture exceeds fees + measured adverse selection?
   **If not, stop here** - that is a valid, cheap result.
3. **Simulate** - Avellaneda–Stoikov with inventory skew and toxicity filters
   on the recordings, with the queue model; walk-forward; the same
   significance discipline as AlgoEdge.
4. **Paper quote** - live quotes computed, not sent; fills inferred from the
   real tape.
5. **Tiny live** - $200-500 margin, minimum size, hard caps, one market.

## 8. Honest expectation

Retail market making is one of the hardest strategies to make pay: on liquid
markets the spread is smaller than the fee, and on illiquid ones you are paid
the spread precisely because the flow is toxic. Step 2 exists so that this is
discovered for the cost of a VPS and two weeks of recording, not for the cost
of the margin.
