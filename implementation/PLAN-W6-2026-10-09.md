### What your screenshots actually show

|---|---|---|

Each symbol you have on two strategies shows **two** rows on demo and **one**
on live. That is the whole symptom, stated precisely: on live, the second slot
for those symbols produced nothing today.

### What I checked and ruled out

- **The scan loop does not deduplicate by symbol.** `bot_service` builds
  `_slots_by_symbol` and then extends `effective_slots` with *every* matching
  slot, so two slots on one symbol are both scanned. That code is correct.
- **`slot_id` is not derived from the symbol.** The legacy migration keys it on
  `symbol:strategy_id`, so two strategies on one symbol get different ids and
  cannot collide in `self.engines` or in the slot book.
- **The duplicate button re-ids.** `duplicateSlot` assigns a fresh `newSlotId()`.

### The hypothesis I would test first

**Risk per trade is $187.93 on live against $309.65 on demo**, and the engine's
own rejection for a stop too wide for the money available is
`below_broker_min_lot`. I hit exactly that rejection repeatedly while testing
today on Crash 1000. This is the same wall as the measured finding already in
the notes: at a small balance the minimum lot makes some instruments
untradeable, and it bites **per slot per instrument**, so it can silence one
strategy on a symbol while another on the same symbol keeps trading.

I cannot confirm it from here — I have no access to the live box's config or
logs, and the two accounts are on different Deriv servers with their own
synthetic feeds, so "the same setup should have appeared" is not something I
can assert either.

### So I built the thing that answers it — `GET /api/slot-diagnostics`

The real gap is that **the system could not answer "why is this slot not
trading?"**. Three completely different situations looked identical from
outside. They are now separated, per configured slot, per day:

| `verdict` | what it means | where to look |
|---|---|---|
| `not scanned` | the slot is not running at all | config: `enabled`, and whether the bot was restarted after you added it |
| `no setup found` | it is scanning; the strategy finds nothing | the market. The honest answer most of the time |
| `every signal refused` | it found setups and something refused all of them | `dropped`, which names what, with counts |
| `running but not in the saved config` | a stale engine from before an edit | restart the bot |

`dropped` uses the same shape as the backtester's rejection funnel, so you can
diff a live slot against a backtest of the same slot directly. If the live box
reports `every signal refused` with `below_broker_min_lot` against those two
slots, the hypothesis is confirmed and the fix is a smaller risk percent or a
larger balance on that account — not a code change.

**To run it:** sign in on the live box and open `/api/slot-diagnostics`, or
compare both boxes from the one front end using the server picker.

## 6.1 The three agent modes, and which exist

You described three ways an agent can relate to a strategy. Here is what is
built and what is not, plainly.

| Mode | What it is | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **A — own strategy** | no base strategy; the agent finds its own entries, states them as a charter, you approve it | **built** |
| **B — trained on an existing strategy** | the agent learns VWAP / ORB / Drift & Jump *as its starting point* and optimises when to take and when to leave those setups | **built** |
| **C — supervisor** | the rule-based strategy opens; the agent may only close or tighten | **built** |

Mode B is the one you care most about and it is the one I have not built. I am
not going to describe it as partly done: there is no code path today that takes
`VWAP_v1` as an agent's base.

### What mode B needs (design)

The pieces mostly exist; the missing part is a base-strategy channel into the
env.

1. **`AgentSpec.base_strategy`.** Empty is mode A. Set, it names a registered
   strategy, and an unregistered name is refused at construction.
2. **The base strategy's signals become the agent's candidate set.** Instead of
   proposing on every bar, the policy sees only the bars where `VWAP_v1`
   produced a signal, plus that signal's own gate vector as extra observation
   columns. Its action space narrows to **take / skip**, with R:R as a second
   output — which is exactly the optimisation you described.
3. **The baseline is the strategy itself, unmodified.** `env.baseline_episode()`
   runs the same signal list through the engine with no policy at all, and the
   gate has a new check — **"beats its base strategy"** — plus a second,
   **"takes enough of its base's setups"**, because an agent that declines
   almost everything has not optimised the strategy, it has switched it off.
4. **The charter writes itself against the base.** "VWAP_v1, taking tier A and
   B setups only, at 1:2.5 instead of 1:3, skipping the Asian session" is a
   readable charter and a true one.
5. **Distillation is easier, not harder** — the gates are the base strategy's
   own named confluences, which `GateRecorder` already stamps on every signal.

One thing worth knowing: most strategies here need two or three timeframes, so
`base_signals.derive_missing` builds any higher timeframe the base strategy
wants from the primary bars, through the resampler that **drops the bar still
forming**. An H1 bar built from the M5 bars available at 10:20 is not the 10:00
H1 bar, and handing it over would be 55 minutes of lookahead. A LOWER timeframe
cannot be derived and is refused rather than guessed.

## 6.2 Supervised learning for direction, RL for management

You asked whether to merge them. **Yes, and the split you proposed is the right
one** — it matches what the literature actually supports rather than what is
fashionable:

- **RL does not clearly beat supervised learning at calling direction.** A 2022
  comparative study found supervised methods ahead on crypto. Direction is a
  prediction problem and prediction problems want a predictor.
- **RL's real edge is cost-aware sequential decisions** — hold, exit, scale.
  That is a control problem, and control problems want a controller.

So: **a supervised model proposes the direction; an RL policy decides whether
to take it, and when to leave.** Your example — the trade runs a long way in
profit, then starts rejecting back, and the agent cuts it before it reverses —
is precisely a control problem on top of a prediction.

Two things to be careful about, both measurable:

1. **The supervised half needs the same leak discipline.** Its labels are
   forward returns, which is the most leak-prone object in the whole system.
   `leakguard`'s prefix probe covers the features; the shuffled-label control
   covers the pipeline. Both apply unchanged.
2. **Train them in the right order.** Supervised first, frozen, then RL on top
   of its output. Training both at once means the RL half can learn to exploit
   the supervised half's mistakes rather than the market's.

**Built** — `backend/rl/director.py`. A regularised logistic regression on the
agent's own features, fitted on the training fold and then frozen, whose
**call** and **confidence** become two extra observation columns. The policy
keeps every market feature it had and gains an opinion alongside, so it can
learn *where* to believe it.

Three structural guards on the label, because the label is literally the
future: `fit` uses only the row index it is given; rows whose label window
reaches past the end of the fold are purged; and the scaler is the agent's own,
not re-fitted here. Logistic regression rather than something deeper for the
same reason the policy is linear — the charter has to state what the agent
does, and the coefficients are that statement.

## 6.3 Which to keep: trained-on-strategy agents, or supervisors?

My answer: **keep mode B, and keep mode C only until mode B exists on the same
strategy.**

The reasoning:

- A **mode B agent subsumes a supervisor.** It decides entry *and* exit on the
  same setups, with one reward and one charter. A supervisor is the same exit
  decision with its hands tied behind its back — it cannot decline a bad entry,
  only cut it afterwards, which is strictly less information acting strictly
  later.
- A supervisor's **only structural advantage is that it is safe by
  construction**: it cannot open anything, so the worst it can do is cut a
  winner. That is a real advantage *while you do not yet trust the agent*.
- So the sequence is: supervise first, because it is the cheapest way to find
  out whether the agent's exit judgement is any good against a baseline you
  already trust. If it survives that, promote it to mode B on the same
  strategy and retire the supervisor pairing.

**On replacing rule-based strategies with agents generally: not yet, and the
reason is in your own numbers.** The measured book has three strategies that
survive real costs, and only `OvernightSession_v1` survives FundedNext's. No
agent has yet been through the §4.6 gate. An agent that has not beaten a rule
on purged out-of-sample folds is not a reason to turn the rule off.

## 6.4 What the agent may decide about risk — built today

You drew the line at R:R, and that is where it sits.

| | agent | risk layer |
|---|---|---|
| direction | **yes** | refuses |
| when to enter | **yes** | refuses |
| when to close, partial-close, tighten | **yes** | honours (it only reduces exposure) |
| **risk : reward** | **yes — built** | the menu is the ceiling |
| **stop distance** | **yes — built** | the menu is the ceiling, plus the 2x-spread floor |
| lot size | **no — not expressible** | decides |
| risk percent | **no — not expressible** | decides |
| daily loss cap, drawdown brake, concurrency | **no — not in its observation** | enforces |

The last three are not rules the agent is trusted to obey. There is no action in
its space that sets them and no feature in its observation that reveals them,
which is a property of the code rather than a promise — `test_rl_features.py`
asserts it.

### Why a stop distance is not a risk control here

It looks like one, so it is worth stating. Lot size is
`risk_dollars / stop_distance`, and `risk_dollars` belongs to the risk layer.
So an agent choosing a **wider** stop buys a **smaller** position — the dollars
at stake are identical whichever stop it picks. It is choosing the *shape* of
the trade, not its *size*.

The two edges where that could still bite are both already closed: a stop
inside 2× the spread is refused by the engine's minimum-stop floor, and a
position too large for the account is clamped by the lot ceiling.

### The menu is the ceiling

Both choices are **indices into a list the admin pre-registers** on the spec:

```
stop_atr_choices = (0.75, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0)
rr_choices       = (1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0)
```

The agent picks a position in that list. There is no action that reaches a
value outside it, and an out-of-range index wraps onto the menu rather than
producing a stop nobody authorised. `risk_head=False` pins both to the slot's
own settings, which is what a supervisor wants — it never opens anything.

Both numbers are written into the charter you approve ("it chooses its own stop
from {0.75x, 1x, 1.5x, 2x, 3x} ATR"), onto every signal's metadata, and into
the Telegram line when a trade opens.

**The lot-size ceiling you described is already how the risk layer works**: it
sizes from the slot's risk percent and clamps to `max_lot_override` and the
broker's limits. An agent cannot raise it because it cannot reach it.

## 6.5 The reward, rebuilt as compartments — built today

You were right that the old shape punished good early exits. It computed
`actual - would-have-made` and subtracted it, so an agent that took +2.4R out
of a +3.0R move was charged -0.6 for being right slightly early. That teaches
it to hold through reversals it can see coming.

The money half is now three questions instead of one:

```
money = realised                      R, signed and UNCAPPED. More return is
                                      always more reward - this is the term
                                      that stops winners being cut, because
                                      holding to 3R always beats cutting at 1R.

      + 0.35 x capture                0..1, never negative. FULL credit for an
                                      exit at or above +1.5R, or at or above
                                      70% of what holding would have made.
                                      Graded down to zero below that. Money
                                      left on the table costs the BONUS, not
                                      the balance.

      + 0.50 x loss_avoided           R of loss cut short. Positive only when
                                      holding would have lost more. The one
                                      thing an exit head is unambiguously for.
```

And, following your later note, **what is deducted rather than merely
withheld**:

- **a loss** — `realised` is signed, and `downside_penalty` can weight losses
  above gains;
- **not following the strategy** — the adherence term is now **signed around
  the charter's own floor**: +1 at full adherence, **0 at the floor, −1 at
  zero**. Earning a smaller bonus was not enough, because a policy could
  out-earn its own charter by abandoning it;
- **named breaches** — an explicit, auditable list (`outside_charter`,
  `exit_not_in_charter`, `out_of_scope`) carried on each trade, so a reduced
  reward says *why* in words.

A trade whose setup the approved charter does not describe is **not taken** —
an unapproved setup must not reach the market — but the **decision is charged**
in training, so the policy learns not to want it.

`left_on_table` and `exit_quality` are still reported as diagnostics; they just
no longer reach the money.

## 6.6 Watching the market every second instead of every minute

You are right that a minute is a long time. Here is the honest trade-off.

**What makes the current design trustworthy is that backtest and live see the
identical bar sequence** — `BarFeed` guarantees it, and that guarantee is
bar-level. There is no tick-level equivalent, and this project has already paid
for divergence between the two halves more than once.

So a per-second exit is not a config change; it is a second, unverifiable
decision path. What I would do instead, in order of preference:

1. **A lower timeframe for the exit head.** An M1 exit head on an M5 entry is
   five times the resolution, keeps `BarFeed` parity exactly, is backtestable,
   and needs no new machinery — just a second timeframe on the spec. This gets
   most of what you want.
2. **A tick-level *safety* layer that is not learned** — a hard "give back no
   more than X of peak profit" rule, evaluated on every price update. Not an
   agent, not trained, trivially testable, and it addresses the specific case
   you described (a long profitable run starting to reverse).
3. **A learned per-tick exit.** Only once there is a tick-replay harness that
   can reproduce it, because until then its backtest does not predict live.

My recommendation is 1 and 2 together. 1 is in the plan as a spec field; 2 is
small and I think genuinely worth doing on its own merits.

## 6.7 Telegram, for when nobody is looking — built today

`backend/rl/notify.py`, wired to the existing `telegram_service`:

| Event | Why it is on the list |
|---|---|
| training finished | with seeds, Sharpe dispersion and the best fold's result — enough to decide whether to look now or in the morning |
| the gate ran | pass or fail, with the failed checks named |
| a charter is waiting for approval | it cannot trade live until you decide |
| **an agent suspended itself for drift** | it stopped trading a strategy you approved; alongside the email |
| a trade opened | direction, symbol, **the R:R it chose**, tier, adherence |
| a close, a partial, a stop tightened | with the gates that fired, and the owner when supervising |

Not sent: every bar, every signal, every scan. A channel that fires constantly
is a channel nobody reads.

Every send is guarded — a notification failure can never change what the
trading system does.
